    Arrested, or “secured”?

    On the police's own news site, a third of the pages about a suspect use the word “diamankan” (secured). In Tribunnews a quarter. In Kompas it rose from 5% (2011) to 15% (2024). In Tempo: 2–8%.

    5% → 15% of Kompas pages about a suspect use “diamankan”, 2011 → 2024

    In Indonesian crime news, people are not always arrested. Some are “diamankan”: secured. The criminal procedure code regulates arrest and detention, and the word “diamankan” is not in it; the softer word is common in police press releases and press conferences. Each newsroom takes it up in a different measure.

    The police's word, publisher by publisher

    Only stories that name the police and a perpetrator, suspect or alleged offender are counted. Each row has two dots: how often diamankan turns up, and how often a word for arrest does.

    What this cannot tell you

    What is counted is pages, not sentences; a page can hold both words. We read 200 places drawn at random: in 156 (78%) what is “diamankan” is a person suspected of an offence, in 42 it is evidence, in 2 something else. So about a fifth of the figures here are not about people, and that is similar in each publisher (about people: Tribunnews 83%, Kompas 70%, the police's site 71%). The word “mengamankan” is not counted. The police's site has enough pages only from 2016; in 2024 every publisher has few. This does not say why: a reporter may use the word because a release is quoted, out of habit, or because the story is about evidence.
