    No viral, no police?

    In 2010–2014 the word “viral” is absent from news about the police. Now it is on 6–12% of those pages, two to three times the rate of all news. Village heads and school heads follow: 2–10%. On pages about the KPK, parliament, ministers and governors: below average.

    6–12% of pages about the police carry the word “viral”

    “No viral, no justice.” Many Indonesians are sure the police only move once a case is loud on social media. Counting words cannot prove that. What can be counted: how often is news about the police also news about something viral?

    Thirteen words for officials and state employees who deal with the public face to face. Each dot is an outlet: of its pages containing the word on the left, what share also contain viral? The hollow rings mark 2010–2014.

    What this cannot tell you

    This is not proof of cause. It cannot say the police acted because something went viral. It only counts pages holding the word “polisi” and the word “viral” together; which came first, the video or the response, is not visible.

    Cases handled without ever going viral, and cases never reported, are not here.

    Crime news is where the videos are. The police are who gets called when a video shows a crime: part of this number is only that.

    The comparison is not zero. The word “viral” rose in all news, which is why the comparison is the row “all pages”.

    A phrase counts as a word of its own. One with a Wikipedia title of its own (“video viral”, “tindak pidana korupsi”) is not counted under the single word. So the figures here err low, not high.

    Who is counted. Twenty-two words for state officials, offices and institutions; those outside the state are not drawn. Outside the state some are viral more often than the police: pages about ojol (app-based motorcycle taxi drivers) hold “viral” on 10%, 7.2% and 28%; satpam (security guards) on 9.8%, 8.6% and 18%; sopir (drivers) on 9.1%, 8.3% and 12%. Tempo has only 109 “kades” pages, so that 5.5% may be far off.
